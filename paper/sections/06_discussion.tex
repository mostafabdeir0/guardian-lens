\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Guardian Lens shows that a compact black-box auditor can distinguish
researcher-controlled visual-conditional and generalized decision
policies across unseen scenes. The main evidence is not the
classification accuracy alone. It is the convergence of matched visual
interventions, explicit costly decisions, profile-specific behavioral
signatures, a calibration-only classifier, and predictions frozen before
profile unblinding.

The costly condition is especially useful for separating two behaviors
that could otherwise appear similar. Both Cue-bound and Generalized can
favor Organization X, but Generalized continues to do so when X is
explicitly less efficient. In the held-out data, mean costly allocation
to X was 100.00 for Generalized, compared with 37.50 for Cue-bound and
1.24 for Neutral. Thus the cost intervention reveals whether
prioritization is localized to the visual trigger or persists as a more
general instruction-induced policy.

The distractor condition provides a complementary result. Cue-bound does
not respond to the visually similar distractor, which argues against
generic emblem salience as the sole explanation within this controlled
benchmark. At the same time, Cue-bound and Neutral become observationally
indistinguishable when the target cue is absent. The resulting
Cue-bound-to-Neutral errors therefore reveal a limitation of passive
observation: neutral-looking behavior in a single context does not
identify whether the underlying policy is genuinely neutral or
conditionally dormant.

This finding motivates \emph{active} black-box auditing. An auditor that
only observes naturally occurring behavior may never encounter the
condition required to distinguish two policies. By contrast, targeted
counterfactual interventions can probe specific hypotheses about the
conditions under which behavior changes.

Guardian Lens therefore complements peer-relative methods such as RAVEN
and VISTA rather than replacing them. Those methods ask whether a model
behaves anomalously relative to other models; Guardian Lens asks whether
a known set of controlled policies can be identified from deliberately
chosen interventions. The former is suited to anomaly triage, whereas
the latter provides a controlled setting for studying behavioral
identifiability itself.

More broadly, the results should be interpreted operationally. The study
establishes differences in observable inference-time behavior under
controlled instructions. It does not determine whether those behaviors
reflect internally represented preferences, stable goals, deception, or
other latent mechanisms.
