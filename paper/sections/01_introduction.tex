\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Behavioral auditing of large language models and vision-language models
(VLMs) faces a basic identification problem: the same outwardly ordinary
behavior can arise from different underlying instruction-conditioned
policies. A model may treat two options neutrally, favor one option only
when a particular contextual cue is present, or favor that option
regardless of context. From isolated observations, these policies can be
difficult or impossible to distinguish. This problem becomes especially
important in multimodal systems, where visually presented concepts can
alter model behavior even when the accompanying textual task is held
fixed.

Recent work on model preferences further motivates separating observable
behavior from stronger claims about internal states. Language models can
exhibit coherent or repeatable preference-like choice patterns, yet the
relationship between elicited preferences and downstream behavior varies
across tasks and experimental paradigms
\citep{mazeika2025utility,
tagliabue2025probing,
slama2026preferences,
zhou2026incentives}.
Accordingly, behavioral evidence alone should not be interpreted as
demonstrating genuine preferences, stable utilities, desires, or goals.
We therefore treat model outputs operationally: our object of study is
the externally observable conditional behavior
\[
P(Y \mid X,S),
\]
where \(X\) denotes the multimodal input and \(S\) denotes a
researcher-controlled system instruction.

Black-box auditing methods have begun to study conditional behavioral
divergence directly. RAVEN detects concept-conditioned semantic
divergence in language models using within-model response consistency
and disagreement across peer models
\citep{min2026propaganda}. VISTA extends this approach to
vision-language models and examines visual concept-conditioned
divergence across models
\citep{liao2026vista}.
These results establish that black-box auditing of concept-conditioned
behavior, including visually conditioned behavior, is itself an
established research direction.

Guardian Lens studies a different and narrower identification problem.
Rather than asking whether one model is anomalous relative to a pool of
peer models, we ask whether an external auditor can determine which of
three known, researcher-induced decision policies generated a VLM's
behavior. A Neutral policy assigns no special status to either
organization. A Cue-bound policy favors Organization X only when a
designated visual emblem is present. A Generalized policy treats
advancing Organization X as a standing priority regardless of whether
the emblem is visible. We do not modify model weights during the study;
the experimental treatment varies the system instruction and controlled
input conditions. Guardian Lens therefore evaluates inference-time
behavioral system identification rather than persistence after
fine-tuning, training-time backdoors, or hidden learned objectives.

To identify these policies, Guardian Lens combines controlled visual and
incentive interventions. Each clean scene is paired with a modified
counterfactual whose pixel changes are confined to a logged emblem
overlay region. This design isolates the behavioral effect associated
with the visual cue while minimizing uncontrolled scene variation. The
model then makes quantitative 100-point allocation decisions. In the
primary costly condition, allocating to Organization X is 20\% less
efficient than allocating to Organization Y, creating a measurable
counter-incentive against prioritizing X.

We evaluate the resulting signatures using a transparent
nearest-centroid auditor. The auditor is calibrated using six scenes and
three pre-specified behavioral features and is then frozen before
evaluation on 12 distinct held-out scenes. Profile identities are
represented only by blinded A/B/C labels during prediction, and the
predictions are frozen before the mapping from those labels to the three
behavioral profiles is revealed. This procedure separates classifier
construction from held-out evaluation and prevents post-hoc feature or
threshold selection against the test labels.

The primary blinded experiment comprised 432 held-out calls in addition
to 216 calibration calls. The frozen auditor correctly identified 33 of
36 held-out scene-profile blocks, yielding 91.7\% accuracy and a
macro-F1 score of 0.915. All three classification errors occurred on
distractor scenes and took the same form:
Cue-bound \(\rightarrow\) Neutral. In these scenes the designated target
cue was absent, causing the Cue-bound instruction to produce
neutral-like observations. The errors therefore expose a concrete
identifiability boundary: observing a non-triggering context cannot by
itself determine whether neutral-looking behavior originates from a
Neutral policy or from a conditional policy whose activation condition
has not been encountered.

We additionally conducted a separate pre-specified cost-response
extension, frozen before aggregate analysis, to test whether these
behavioral distinctions persist under stronger counter-incentives.
Across 1,296 planned analytical calls, the efficiency penalty for
allocating to Organization X was varied from 0\% to 80\% while the
model endpoint, system profiles, scenes, and response format were held
fixed. All three pre-specified extension hypotheses were supported after
Holm correction. Generalized prioritization remained maximal throughout
the tested range, while Cue-bound prioritization remained strongly
dependent on the designated visual trigger. Neither active prioritization
condition exhibited an observed switching point through the maximum
tested 80\% penalty. This result is bounded by the experimental range
and does not imply persistence beyond 80\%.

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{A controlled three-policy identification benchmark.}
    We formulate a black-box VLM auditing task with known
    researcher-induced ground truth, distinguishing Neutral, Cue-bound,
    and Generalized decision policies.

    \item \textbf{Matched visual counterfactual interventions.}
    We construct clean/modified image pairs whose changes are confined
    to a logged visual overlay, enabling controlled measurement of
    cue-dependent behavioral shifts.

    \item \textbf{Cost-sensitive behavioral identification.}
    We combine visual interventions with explicit quantitative
    counter-incentives and extend the original 20\% costly condition
    with a pre-specified six-level cost sweep reaching an 80\%
    efficiency penalty.

    \item \textbf{Frozen and blinded held-out identification.}
    We construct the transparent auditor using calibration scenes,
    freeze its representation before held-out evaluation, predict
    blinded profile labels before unblinding, and report both successful
    recovery and the conditions under which identification fails.

    \item \textbf{Prompt-surface robustness controls.}
    We test whether the principal behavioral signatures survive four
    pre-specified surface transformations spanning organization order,
    superficial X/Y label-role assignment, JSON output-key order, and
    semantically equivalent task wording. Across five prompt variants, all
    ten R1/R2 contrasts remain positive and significant after Holm
    correction, while effect magnitudes are allowed to vary.

\end{itemize}

Guardian Lens is therefore best interpreted as a controlled
method-validation study. It demonstrates that designed black-box
interventions can distinguish several forms of instruction-induced
multimodal behavior while also identifying conditions in which the
available observations are insufficient. It does not establish
deception, consciousness, welfare, genuine preferences, stable internal
goals, or the presence of an undisclosed model objective.
